================================================================================
  Surface Model — The Rendered Surface
  The surface a body has IS the surface the renderer draws
================================================================================
  Nov 2026 · companion to design.txt / movement.txt / landing.txt / positioning.txt.
  This document is the canonical reference for WHAT the surface is and WHY
  everything that touches it (the walker, the picker, every craft) agrees with
  the pixels. It explains the model that replaced the old "per-cell sphere"
  walk surface, and it states the invariants that keep it true as the ground
  moves.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The canonical surface of a body is the RENDERED top surface — the
  chord-triangle fans of its hex elements. Nothing that walks, picks, parks,
  lands, launches, or reports altitude may resolve a body's surface to
  anything else.

  There is exactly ONE surface, and it is the one on screen:
    * the walker's feet ride it,
    * the camera's eye sits one body-height above it,
    * a raycast (picking) stops on it,
    * a craft's altitude, its park anchor, and its launch pad are measured
      from it,
    * the HUD's altitude number is read off it.

  The invariant that makes this possible: the drawn fan and the walked
  surface are DERIVED FROM THE SAME NUMBERS. geometry.js draws each cell's
  top as a triangle fan of (center + corners), every vertex at radius
  R + h(cell) with INSET = 0 (so adjacent tops share their edge exactly —
  the surface is seamless). fanRadiusAt() (core/surface.js) queries that same
  fan analytically. They are the same object, so they cannot disagree. If the
  ground is painted, deformed, or shaken, both the mesh and the surface
  re-derive from the new elements — in the same frame — so the feet and the
  pixels move together (see §7).

  The one surface is STAR-SHAPED: every ray from the body's center meets it
  exactly once. Consequences that the rest of the engine leans on:
    * every direction has a unique surface radius — "the ground under dir"
      is well-defined, so a walk view's horizon is honest (you see the ground
      that is actually there, at the distance it actually is);
    * every fall terminates — a body-relative "altitude → 0" always has an
      answer, which is what makes "a craft lands when its altitude hits 0"
      and "an EVA fall ends on foot" physical statements rather than UI ones;
    * picking is a single signed function of the ray parameter — no trimesh,
      no spatial structures, no per-triangle lookups.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHY NOT A SPHERE — THE BUG THIS MODEL REPLACES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The obvious stand-in for "the surface" is a per-cell sphere: r(dir) =
  R + h(cell(dir)) — the radius of the flat top, extruded to a full sphere
  around each hex. It is wrong in a way that is invisible up close and
  catastrophic for the walk view:

  A hex top is a flat CHORD fan, not a spherical cap. The fan's center sits
  at R + h, but its corners and edges are CHORDS — they lie INSIDE the
  sphere of radius R + h. A flat fan sags below that sphere by, at worst,
  apothem²/8R (the sag of a flat chord face under a sphere of the same
  radius). On a true-scale 35 km hex grid that is ~100 m; measured at the
  Ceres walker spot (R = 471 km, apothem ≈ 20 km) it was 137.6 m.

  While the walker rode the sphere, the 1.6 m astronaut's feet sat on
  R + h — and the GROUND UNDER ITS FEET (the drawn fan) was ~100–140 m
  BELOW that. The astronaut floated a hundred times its own height over the
  ground it was standing on. Because the eye was high, the walk view read as
  "looking at the ground from way up in the sky": the mottled ground a couple
  of degrees below the horizon was 2–3 km distant, while a 90 m craft shared
  the sphere's view height and so also read as far off. The horizon, the
  parallax, and the sense of scale were all corrupted by the same split —
  the eye on one surface, the ground on another.

  Fixing it at the source (walk the fan) removed the split entirely: the eye
  is on the fan, so the ground the astronaut sees IS the ground it stands on,
  at the true distance. No micro-detail disc, no horizon fade — those were
  bandages for a surface that should not have been there. The lesson is the
  model: ONE surface, and it is the rendered one.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE FAN MODEL — fanRadiusAt
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The walked surface is the fan. For a body-LOCAL unit direction dir that
  falls in cell c (c = nearest cell center — the Voronoi lookup), the surface
  radius along dir is the LOWER ENVELOPE of the cell's fan-triangle planes:

      r(dir) = min_k  rho · (n_k · c) / (n_k · dir)          (1)

  where rho = R + h(c) is the cell's current vertex radius, c is the cell's
  center direction, and n_k is the unit normal of fan-triangle k (the triangle
  C–E_k–E_{k+1}). Each fan triangle is a flat plane; the fan is their lower
  envelope, so (1) returns the exact point where dir meets the drawn fan.

  Three properties make (1) the right tool:

  EXACT. (1) equals the exact Möller–Trumbore ray/triangle intersection of
  dir against the real fan, to machine precision (6.6e-16) for every
  direction in the cell, across all cells, frequencies 1–24 (including the
  twelve pentagons). And at the exact cell-EDGE directions — where a single
  cell's triangles only meet in a shared line and a naive triangle test
  degenerates — the min still returns the shared edge's radius. The envelope
  is MORE robust than per-triangle intersection at the one place intersection
  is weakest.

  CHEAP. (1) is allocation-free: ~6 dot products and divisions per query.
  That matters because it runs at 60 Hz in the walker AND inside
  intersectHeightfield, which calls the surface ~1000 times per pointer event
  (every pointer move AND every wheel tick raycasts). A mesh raycast or a
  heap-allocating formula here would show up in the frame time.

  FREE TO DEFORM. The plane ORIENTATION n_k depends only on the grid (the
  chord directions E_k − C, E_{k+1} − C), which depend only on `frequency` —
  never on h. So the normals + (n_k · c) are computed ONCE per frequency and
  cached (fanTables: frequency → { normals, centerCos }). Only the plane's
  DISTANCE from the center scales with rho = R + h. A paint stroke, a
  deformation, or a simulated earthquake changes h — it costs nothing but the
  multiply in (1). This is the entire reason "the walker moves with the
  shaking ground" is true by construction rather than by extra code (§7).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE TWO QUANTITIES — do not conflate them
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The surface model keeps two different numbers straight, and mixing them is
  the bug this section exists to prevent:

  h(cell) — the per-element RELIEF. The stored height of one hex, in world
  units. This is the quantity the step/wall logic compares: a neighbor whose
  h differs by more than stepUp is a WALL (the move is blocked and slides
  along it); a drop of more than stepDownSnap is a real FALL; a smaller step
  is climbed or snapped down. h is the terrain the walker NAVIGATES.

  fan radius = fanRadiusAt(dir) — the POSITION radius the walker and every
  craft ride. It is CONTINUOUS inside a cell (flat, sagging from R + h at the
  cell's center down toward its edges) and JUMPS by the relief delta across a
  cell edge. It is the terrain the walker STANDS on.

  The rule: step/wall logic compares h, never the in-cell fan sag. The sag
  within a single cell is a fraction of a tile — far below stepUp and
  stepDownSnap — so it must not read as a step or a fall. Only the RELIEF
  delta at a shared edge is terrain. Conversely, position and altitude always
  use the fan radius, never R + h. Equal-h neighbors are continuous (they
  share a chord); different-h neighbors step by exactly the h difference —
  which is exactly what the drawn mesh does.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. EVERYONE USES THE SAME SURFACE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The single surface is shared by every consumer, through a small number of
  functions that are the ONLY places a body's surface is resolved:

  WALKER (core/surface.js)
    heightAt(dir).radius = fanRadiusAt(...) — the feet ride the fan; the
    camera sits at feet + metrics.eye along the local radial. The step/wall
    logic compares per-cell h. The walk view's ground is the fan.

  PICKING (core/surface.js intersectHeightfield)
    The analytic ray→surface march evaluates the signed penetration
      f(t) = |origin + t·dir| − fanRadiusAt(direction of that point)
    and finds its first zero. A hit snaps radially onto the hit cell's fan so
    it never floats. This is what makes a paint stroke land on the drawn
    tile, at the drawn height — and it is LOD-independent and
    cell-count-independent (the cost does not grow with 500k cells).

  CRAFT — ALTITUDE (core/motion.js altitudeOf)
    Altitude of a world position above a body's RENDERED surface:
    d − fanRadiusAt(...) when the body's elements are in hand, flat-sphere
    otherwise. This is the HUD's terrain-corrected altitude (refreshed every
    6th frame — a cell search — the cheap flat number the rest). It is what a
    pilot SEES as "how high am I over the ground" — over the mountains, not
    over the invisible sphere.

  CRAFT — PARK / LAUNCH (core/motion.js heightAtDir, parkCraft,
  launchCraftState)
    A parked craft's anchor radius is r = body.radius + heightAtDir(dir) —
    the rendered fan under its dir. Launch lifts from that fan-resolved pad
    by LAUNCH_LIFT·R. So a craft parks ON the drawn ground (a mountain top or
    a valley floor) and launches from it — not on the sphere.

  THE CHEAP TRIGGER vs THE RESOLVED POSITION (the subtle, important split)
    The per-frame LANDING TRIGGER is deliberately the FLAT sphere:
      d − body.radius <= LAND_EPS · body.radius      (LAND_EPS = 0.002)
    and the EVA fall ends at d − body.radius <= 0.005 · body.radius. These
    are cheap (no cell lookup) and robust: a craft descending toward a valley
    is not gated by a nearby ridge's fan, and "you are basically at the
    surface" is a clean, terrain-independent signal. But the MOMENT the
    trigger fires, the position RESOLVES to the rendered fan (parkCraft, and
    the walker on foot). The pattern, stated once: the sphere is only the
    cheap "close enough" tripwire; the fan is where everything actually
    LANDS, STANDS, and is MEASURED from. Never let a resolved or persisted
    position fall back to the sphere — that is the §2 bug returning.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. DYNAMIC SURFACES — the ground is expected to change
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The model is built on the expectation that a body's surface is DYNAMIC: it
  is painted, deformed, or shaken at runtime, and the walker and every craft
  must track it while they traverse it or avoid it. This is not an edge case
  to be patched — it is the operating assumption, and the model makes it
  costless:

  * The surface is RE-DERIVED from planet.elements on every query. heightAt
    and fanRadiusAt read the current hs array; there is no cached surface
    radius that can go stale. If h changes, the very next read sees it.

  * The surface memo and the mesh rebuild on the SAME identity change. The
    walked surface (makeSurface) and the drawn geometry are both memoized on
    `planet.elements` (and `frequency`) identity — the copy-on-write contract
    shared with everything else. A terrain edit replaces the elements array;
    the memo rebuilds the surface AND the mesh in the same frame. Feet and
    pixels move together. There is no window in which the mesh shows one
    surface and the walker rides another.

  * Deforming h is free; reshaping the grid is not. Because the fan plane
    orientations are cached per frequency (§3), a change to h (paint,
    deformation, an earthquake that raises or lowers tiles) costs nothing but
    the rho rescale in (1). Only a change to `frequency` (a different grid)
    rebuilds the fanTables cache. Shaking — thousands of tiles' h changing
    per frame — is exactly the cheap case.

  * The flyer tracks a moving surface the same way. A craft's altitude
    (altitudeOf) and a parked craft's anchor (heightAtDir) read the current
    elements every frame, so a craft parked on a shaking ridge rides it, and
    a craft in descent sees the true, current altitude over the terrain it is
    about to meet. "Navigating the rendered surface as it traverses, or
    avoids, a body" is the same fanRadiusAt the walker uses — there is no
    separate surface for flight.

  The consequence worth stating plainly: because the walked surface and the
  drawn surface are the same derivation of the same elements, you cannot
  paint the ground under a standing astronaut and have them keep standing on
  the old ground. They move with it. That is the requirement, and it holds
  without any extra synchronization code.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. THE BODY-LOCAL FRAME — and the measurement gotcha
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The surface is queried in the body's LOCAL frame: a direction dir + a
  radius r, with the world pose composed as planetPos + quat·(dir·r) from the
  live registry. The walker and a parked craft store their pose this way, so
  they ride the body's spin and orbit for free — a standing astronaut's
  body-local pose is unchanged even as the planet spins and flies.

  MEASUREMENT GOTCHA (bought the hard way). Live body positions in the
  registry are FLOAT32. At Sol-Real distances (|pos| ~ 4.2e6 units) the
  float32 quantum is ~0.25 units ≈ 9 km. So any measurement of "how high is
  the eye above the ground" taken in WORLD space against a registry position
  is dominated by that 9 km quantum and is meaningless at meter scale. To
  verify the weld (or measure eye height) you MUST work in the body-LOCAL
  frame: transform the camera through the registry's inverse quaternion
  against the SAME quantized center the camera was composed from, and read the
  local radius. probe-walk-fan.mjs does exactly this, and it is the only way
  the "feet 0.0000 m above the fan" result is real. Do not "simplify" a
  surface measurement into world space — it will silently read as a 9 km
  error and the weld will look broken when it is not.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. WHY THIS IS CHEAP ENOUGH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The surface model's whole design pressure is: it must be exact AND cheap,
  because it runs in the two hottest paths in the app.

  * fanRadiusAt is ~6 dot products + divisions, no allocation, no grid
    rebuild (fanTables cached per frequency).
  * The 60 Hz walker calls it once per step; the pick sweep calls it ~1000×
    per pointer event. A 5000-pick benchmark on a 492-cell world runs in ~12 ms.
  * intersectHeightfield is a signed-function march on a sphere chord —
    LOD-independent and cell-count-independent. Picking a 500k-cell giant
    costs the same as a 92-cell moon.
  * The per-frame craft landing trigger is the flat sphere (one distance),
    not a cell lookup — the cell search only happens when the trigger fires
    (park) or for the HUD's every-6th-frame terrain altitude.

  The trade, stated honestly: exactness to the rendered mesh is what buys the
  honest walk view (§2). The cost of that exactness is a handful of dot
  products — which is why the model is min-of-planes (1) instead of a mesh
  raycast. Do not "upgrade" the surface query to a trimesh or a per-triangle
  structure to save a dot product; the fan envelope is the cheap exact answer.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. VERIFICATION & GUARDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The model is pinned in several places; a regression in any of them is a
  real bug, not a tolerance to loosen:

  * min-of-planes == exact triangle intersection (6.6e-16, all cells, freqs
    1–24). Verified directly against Möller–Trumbore on the real grid. If
    (1) stops matching the triangle hit, the fan formula is wrong.
  * scripts/test-surface.mjs — walker weld, step/wall, falls, and the
    analytic-pick vs brute-force-march agreement. The march-agreement test
    verifies sub-step slivers: a fan KINK (the slope discontinuity where two
    flat fan planes meet at a cell edge) can create a grazing penetration
    thinner than the march step; a reported hit is then checked to actually
    penetrate at its own point, and a confirmed sliver counts as a documented
    miss (a non-penetrating "hit" is still a failure). Do not weaken this.
  * scripts/probe-walk-fan.mjs — the regression probe for THIS model, on
    true-scale Ceres in the body-local frame (§7): feet welded to the fan
    (machine precision), eye a real 1.6 m human above the fan, parked craft
    anchored on the fan. Exits non-zero if the weld breaks. Needs
    sol-real.json (node scripts/gen-sol-real.mjs).
  * scripts/test-headless.mjs — the rendered pipeline: the real-human walker
    welds to 35 km terrain, and both flight→walk chains (launch→park→eject→
    on foot) terminate on the surface. These run the real tree + workers.

  The measured baseline on Sol Real Ceres (the original bug scenario):
    feet above rendered fan   0.00e+0 m   (welded, machine precision)
    eye above rendered fan    1.600 m     (a real human)
    old walking sphere sag    137.6 m     (the astronaut now stands ON it)
    parked craft over ground  -6.25e-11 m (on the drawn ground)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. INVARIANTS — DO NOT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * Never resolve a body's surface (position, altitude, pick, park, launch)
    to the per-cell sphere R + h. The sphere is only the cheap landing
    tripwire; every resolved/persisted position is the rendered fan. This is
    the §2 bug.
  * Never introduce a SECOND surface. There is one surface and it is the
    rendered one. A walk surface, a flight surface, a pick surface that differ
    from the mesh will desynchronize and the walk view will lie again.
  * Never cache a surface RADIUS. Cache only the grid-derived plane
    orientations (per frequency). The radius must be re-derived from the
    current h on every query, or a dynamic surface will not be tracked (§6).
  * Never let step/wall logic compare the in-cell fan sag — compare per-cell
    h only, or the sag reads as phantom steps and falls (§4).
  * Never measure a surface weld in world space at giant-body distances —
    float32 center quantization swamps it. Body-local frame, always (§7).
  * Never route the surface query through a trimesh or per-triangle lookup —
    the analytic fan envelope is the cheap exact answer (§8).
  * Keep core/surface.js and core/motion.js framework-free (no three, no DOM
    at module load) — that is what lets the node tests and the headless
    harness exercise the surface directly.
  * Keep the surface memo and the geometry memo on the SAME identity
    (planet.elements + frequency) so a dynamic surface moves the feet and the
    pixels in the same frame (§6).
