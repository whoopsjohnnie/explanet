================================================================================
  ExPlanet — Landing & Surface Exploration
  Design behind "descend onto a planet and walk around on it"
================================================================================
  Oct 2026 · deep-dive companion to docs/design.txt §12 (short version) and
  README (usage). Code: src/core/surface.js (pure math),
  src/components/Walker.jsx (frame loop), src/components/ui/WalkHud.jsx.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. WHAT IT DOES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Press 🚀 (PlanetList row) or "Land on surface" (Inspector) on any non-star
  world. You drop — in FIRST PERSON, from the astronaut's eyes — above the
  face of the planet currently turned toward you and FALL onto the terrain
  and land. From there you walk the surface with WASD (view-relative),
  sprint with Shift, jump with Space — always bound to the hex terrain:
  contour-following, stepping up low ledges, blocked (and sliding) by tall
  tile walls, falling off cliffs under local gravity. Hold & drag to look
  around. Esc lifts off and hands the camera back to the editor. The walk
  keeps working while the planet orbits and spins, and even while the global
  sim is paused.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 2. THE CENTRAL PROBLEM: STANDING ON A HEX PLANET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The obvious move — drop a cannon rigid body onto the planet and let the
  physics engine sort it out — fails here for two structural reasons:

  * Collider vs surface mismatch. Planet bodies are SPHERES at the base
    radius R (see Planet.jsx). The rendered surface is the star-shaped roof
    of the hex elements: r(dir) = R + h(cell(dir)), a few tiles of relief
    (per-world scale). A physical capsule would sink into every mountain, stand on
    invisible air over valleys, and tumble off the invisible sphere.
    A trimesh collider rebuilt on every terrain edit would be expensive,
    and cannon's worker round-trip makes rebuilds visibly janky.

  * The ground is not inertial. The planet is a fast-orbiting, spinning
    body. A small body standing on a fast-rotating sphere is a classic
    numerical misery: friction drags it, the contact solver ejects it, and
    tunneling scales badly with the size ratio.

  Also required by the brief: movement must follow surface CONTOURS exactly.
  Exactness is easy the moment you stop simulating a body and instead treat
  "standing on this planet" as what it really is for a star-shaped height
  field: a radius that is a FUNCTION of direction.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 3. THE MODEL: A KINEMATIC WALKER ON A STAR-SHAPED SURFACE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  core/surface.js integrates a tiny walker of its own:

    state = { dir: unit vector (planet-local), r: radius, radialVel,
              grounded: bool, cell: index under foot }

  Everything is expressed in the PLANET-LOCAL frame. The world only ever
  sees the composed pose:

    worldPos = planet.pos + planet.quat · (dir · r)

  where planet.pos / planet.quat are read every frame from the live body
  registry (core/physics.js) — the same mutable 60 Hz side-channel the
  gravity loop, the camera follower and the save snapshot already use.

  Consequences:
  * The walker rides the planet's spin and orbital flight FOR FREE: its own
    state never mentions where the planet is in the solar system. Standing
    still on the night side stays on the night side.
  * Walking is exact against the same elements array the renderer builds
    geometry from — the boot always touches the visible tile.
  * The walk is independent of cannon: it works while planets orbit and
    while <Physics isPaused>, which doubles as "explore a frozen world".
  * It stays testable: the entire model is pure math that runs in node.

  This follows the project's architectural rule: live 60 Hz physics lives
  OUTSIDE React state (store.js holds only the walkMode session identity
  { planetId }; the walker object itself is module-mutable state).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4. WHICH TILE IS UNDER FOOT? (VORONOI LOOKUP)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The cell under a direction is the one whose CENTER direction is nearest —
  the Voronoi cell of the geodesic dual grid. Rather than scanning all
  cells, the walker funnels: starting from the previous cell (usually the
  answer already), greedily hop to the neighbor with higher dot(center, dir)
  until no neighbor improves. On a quasi-regular geodesic dual this is a
  handful of steps per frame and has no local maxima; a brute-force
  reference (also used when there is no hint) is cross-checked headlessly
  over thousands of random directions (scripts/test-surface.mjs).

  Height is PIECEWISE CONSTANT per cell — deliberately matching the
  rendered look: the planet IS a mosaic of flat hex plates with wall
  quads between height steps, so "flat plateau, step at tile edges" is
  what the eyes already see.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 5. WALKING RULES: STEPS, WALLS, LEDGES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Each frame, the camera-relative wish vector is projected onto the tangent
  plane at dir and rotates dir by (speed·dt)/r — motion along the sphere.
  The height change into the new cell decides what happens:

    rise ≤ stepUp (0.81 tile)    → step up: r snaps to the new tile roof
    drop ≤ stepDownSnap (0.55) → step down, stay grounded (no stair-jitter)
    bigger drop                → start a real FALL from the old height
    rise  > stepUp             → WALL: the move is blocked, and the wish
                                 component into the wall is removed so you
                                 SLIDE along it instead of sticking

  Airborne you keep 75% air control and may drift over cliff edges (you
  cannot fly: jump height is capped below the tallest wall class). Because
  the surface is a single-valued r(dir), every fall terminates — there are
  no caves or overhangs to fall past, which is what makes a tiny hand-rolled
  integrator completely safe here.

  Walls matter in practice mostly on player-EDITED terrain: natural fBm
  presets vary smoothly enough that neighbors rarely differ by more than
  half a step, so the world is very walkable, while raised cliffs you
  paint become real barriers.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 6. GRAVITY, FALLS AND JUMPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Surface gravity is the SAME softened law as the orbital sim:

    a(r) = G · m / (r² + ε²) · gravityScale,   ε² = 4, scale 0.12
                                          clamped into [1, 30]

  — consistent with stepGravity/orbitalSpeed — scaled for feel (the sim's
  raw G·m values are tuned for 20 s orbits, way too strong for legs) and
  clamped so tiny moons stay fun and giants stay survivable.

    world (starter)      g        fall from spawn    feel
    Jupiter                1        ~1.8 s             long, moonlike
    Earth                  1        ~0.55 s            quick drop
    Pluto                  1        ~0.24 s            a hop

   (All true-scale starter worlds clamp to g = 1: real surface gravity at
   these scene scales sits below the floor — the clamp is what keeps
   walking lively; edited/superdense worlds can pull harder.)

  Jump velocity is derived from a TARGET APEX rather than a fixed impulse:
  v = sqrt(2·g·jumpHeight). Every world then has the same jump HEIGHT
  (~1.9 tiles — i.e. the same fraction of the astronaut, see §9) with
  gravity-appropriate hang time — the one most predictable
  choice for a toy where g varies 10× between bodies. Max fall speed is
  capped (48 tiles/s) so falling off a tall edited cliff can't produce absurd
  frame-to-frame radius jumps.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 7. THE LANDING CHOREOGRAPHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  "When landing we should fall onto it" is honored literally:

  * Spawn direction: the point of the planet currently facing the camera
    (normalize(camPos − planetPos), rotated into the local frame). You
    always land where you were looking.
  * Spawn altitude: createWalker starts the walker at surface + 7.25 tiles
    (surface.metrics.spawnHover), radialVel = 0, airborne. The first thing it
    does is FALL — the camera
    is AT the astronaut's eyes, so the ground rising IS the landing.
    ~0.55 s on the starter's Earth, ~1.8 s on Jupiter,
    then a grounded landing.
  * First-person eye camera, at all times — fall, walk, jump, on a spinning
    orbiting world: every frame the camera is PLACED at feet + up·EYE
    (EYE = surface.metrics.eye = one tile of that planet's grid, so the
    astronaut is always one plate tall) and aimed by lookAt(eye, eye+gaze,
    localUp). The view heading is stored PLANET-LOCAL — exactly like the
    walker's dir + r — and composed every frame as gaze = quat·fwdL, so the
    view is WELDED to the surface: a landmark stays dead ahead while the
    planet spins or flies, and the horizon turns WITH the planet instead of
    sliding under you. (A world-space heading was the naive first cut and
    visibly drifted over the terrain on any rotating world.) The heading
    starts as the editor camera's view direction at takeover, rotated into
    the local frame.
  * Look-around is a held pointer drag (Walker.jsx listens on the canvas):
    yaw rotates the heading around the walker's LOCAL up, pitch around the
    local right, elevation clamped to ±83° so the horizon never flips or
    rolls. This is precisely what OrbitControls cannot do on a spherical
    world — its azimuth is locked to world Y — so OrbitControls is disabled
    for the whole surface session and the walker owns the camera outright.
  * The same heading drives movement: the WASD wish vector is the view
    heading projected onto the tangent plane, so you always walk where you
    look, through the air included (75% air control).
  * Lift-off (unmount): the orbit pivot moves back to the planet center,
    dragging the camera by the same delta, and OrbitControls re-enables for
    normal editing.

  The camera IS the astronaut, so no body is rendered — a self-avatar would
  only clip against the eyes. Painting is guarded while walking, so look-
  drags never paint the planet by accident.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 8. INPUT, UI AND FRAMEWORK INTEGRATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * Keyboard: Walker.jsx owns WASD/arrows/Shift, and Space is EDGE-triggered
    (keydown, not repeat) and consumed per frame as the jump input.
  * Pointer: Walker.jsx listens for pointerdown on the canvas and tracks one
    pointer (capture); pixel deltas accumulate and are consumed once per
    frame by the view rotation — frame-rate independent, works with mouse
    and touch. Right-drag looks too (contextmenu is suppressed).
  * App.jsx's global shortcut handler short-circuits while walkMode is set:
    Space is swallowed (a previously focused button must not be re-clicked
    by the jump key), Esc exits surface mode, Ctrl/Cmd+S still saves.
  * The palette (brushes) is hidden while walking; a WalkHud strip shows the
    world name, grounded/falling state, live altitude & vertical speed,
    the key legend, and a Lift off button.
  * Live HUD numbers come from a tiny mutable telemetry object
    (surface.js walkReadout) that the frame loop writes at 60 Hz and the
    HUD POLLS at ~8 Hz — 60 Hz data never becomes React state.
  * The body registry gained one field: Planet.jsx now also subscribes the
    body QUATERNION, which is what makes the local↔world composition (and
    therefore walking on a spinning planet) possible.
  * Session lifecycle: store.landOnPlanet refuses star presets ("you'd
    vaporize"); walkMode is cleared when a new world loads or is created,
    and when the walked planet is deleted; the frame loop self-exits if the
    planet's registry entry vanishes. Landing on a different planet remounts
    the Walker via key={planetId}.
  * The surface query is memoized on the planet document identity — the
    same copy-on-write contract as the geometry rebuild memo: edit terrain
    while walking (via a second control, e.g. keyboard during a pause) and
    the walker is instantly on the new height field.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 9. TUNING REFERENCE (core/surface.js SURFACE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The astronaut scales WITH its world. Every LENGTH constant below is a
  multiple of the planet's TILE SCALE — the circumradius of one hex plate,
  tile = R·√(8π/(3√3·N)) — resolved once by makeSurface() into
  surface.metrics (world units). The astronaut is always "one tile tall":
  on the true-scale starter Earth's tile is 0.021 u, so the eye sits 0.021
  u up and the horizon dips a uniform ~8° on every world. A FIXED eye
  height cannot do that — 0.62 u on true-scale Earth stands 30 tiles tall
  and tips the horizon 45° down, which made every planet read as a small
  ball (the pre-scaling bug this section exists to prevent re-introducing).

    eye 1.0 · walkSpeed 6.12 · sprintMult 1.8 · airControl 0.75
    stepUp 0.81 · stepDownSnap 0.89
    jumpHeight 1.85 · maxFallSpeed 48.3 · spawnHover 7.25
    (lengths are × tile; sprintMult/airControl are ratios; gravity stays
    in world units: gravityScale 0.12, clamp [1, 30])

  The ratios are the original absolute tuning (eye 0.62, speed 3.8, …)
  divided by a natural grid's tile (0.6204), so scale-1 worlds keep their
  exact old feel. stepUp ≈ 0.8 tiles and jumpHeight ≈ 1.9 tiles preserve
  the original "climbable terrain with real barriers" character; stride,
  sprint-lap time and horizon dip are now identical from a 0.4 u moon to a
  21 u giant (a sprint lap of any starter world ≈ 52 s). Walker.jsx also
  swaps in a walk-mode camera near plane (eye·0.08, floored at 0.002) so
  the small astronaut does not clip the plates under foot, and restores
  the editor's plane on lift-off.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 10. TESTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  All risky logic is in core/surface.js and runs headlessly
  (scripts/test-surface.mjs, wired into npm test — 15 checks):

  * funnel Voronoi lookup == brute-force nearest center, thousands of
    random directions × frequencies, from random hints;
  * heightAt at cell centers equals the element heights;
  * surfaceGravity equals the softened law incl. clamps;
  * spawn → fall → land: grounded within seconds, radius equals surface
    radius, never below the surface during the fall;
  * 25 s sprint: covers real arc length, never dips below the surface,
    stays glued on flat relief, state finite;
  * a synthetically raised tile blocks dead-on approach and lets angled
    approach slide along it (natural fBm terrain has no walls this big —
    they come from player edits);
  * jump leaves the ground, apex lands within tolerance of the target,
    re-lands;
  * determinism (identical scripts → identical states) and a 30 s random
    walk + jumps fuzz with all values finite.

  The R3F half (camera, input, avatar) is verified by build + manual
  browser testing, as everywhere else in this project.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 11. LIMITATIONS & NATURAL NEXT STEPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * One walkable planet at a time (bound to its parent's gravity);
    inter-planetary transfer / spaceflight is a separate feature.
  * Piecewise-constant tiles mean a bumpy (correct) gait between plates —
    a smoothed height interpolation is possible but would misrepresent
    the rendered geometry, which is genuinely flat-topped.
  * Water and lava tiles walk like solid ground; terrain-specific move
    speed / damage is a small addition to stepWalker.
  * No footstep dust, landing squash, or audio — the readout + fall are
    doing the physical storytelling for now.
  * Airborne wish keeps full steering; a jump ARC lock (locked heading)
    would feel more "physical" if ever desired.
  * The camera takeover is a cut; an eased pitch-in / FOV sweep during the
    landing fall would polish the first-person descent further.
